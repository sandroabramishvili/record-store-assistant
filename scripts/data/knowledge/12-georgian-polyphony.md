---
title: Georgian Polyphonic Singing on Record
topic: genre
---
Georgia has one of the world's oldest traditions of polyphonic singing: music for several independent voices sung together. UNESCO recognised Georgian polyphony as a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2001.

**What it sounds like.** Most songs are in three parts. Two solo voices on top often weave around each other, while a group sings a sustained drone or a moving bass line underneath. The harmonies include close, sharp intervals that can sound surprisingly modern to listeners used to Western classical choirs.

**Regional styles.** Songs from western Georgia, such as Guria and Samegrelo, are known for complex, fast-moving counterpoint and yodel-like "krimanchuli" lines. Eastern regions such as Kakheti are famous for long, ornamented table songs over a sustained drone. Mountain regions such as Svaneti preserve some of the oldest-sounding songs.

**Table songs and church music.** Many songs belong to the *supra*, the Georgian feast, where toasts and songs alternate through the evening. Georgian Orthodox chant developed its own polyphonic tradition alongside folk singing.

**On record.** During the Soviet era, the state label Melodiya recorded many Georgian ensembles. Internationally, ensembles such as the Rustavi Choir introduced the style to Western listeners through albums released abroad. Today, groups such as Basiani continue the tradition with new recordings.

**Listening tip:** play these records at a comfortable volume in a quiet room and listen for how the voices move against each other rather than for a single melody.
